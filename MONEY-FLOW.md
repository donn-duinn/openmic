# Part 1 — What venues pay, and to whom

## 1.1 Victorian state government

### Gaming machine (pokies) tax — the big one

Set by statute in the **Gambling Regulation Act 2003 (Vic) s 3.6.6C**. Tax is calculated
**per gaming machine entitlement, per calendar month**, on the *average revenue per gaming
machine entitlement* (total venue gaming revenue for the month divided by the average number
of connected machines). The rate is **marginal** — each band applies only to the part of
average monthly revenue falling in it.

**Table for months after August 2022** — s 3.6.6C(6), the schedule currently in force:

| Part of average monthly revenue per entitlement | Club rate | Hotel (pub) rate |
|---|---|---|
| Does not exceed $2,666 | **0.00%** | **8.33%** |
| Exceeds $2,666, up to $6,666.67 | 46.70% | 55.03% |
| Exceeds $6,666.67, up to $12,500 | 51.17% | 57.50% |
| Exceeds $12,500 | 60.67% | 65.00% |

Tax is payable to the Commission within **7 days** of the end of the calendar month
(s 3.6.6C(10)). Section 3.6.6D required the Treasurer to review these rates after 16 August
2023 and report by 16 February 2024.

**The structural point:** the first $2,666 per machine per month is taxed at 8.33% for a
hotel and **0.00% for a club**. That 8.33% differential is not an accident — it is exactly
the percentage that funds the Community Support Fund (see Part 2.1). Hotels pay it as tax;
clubs are instead required to *spend* the equivalent themselves.

### The club side: required community benefit contribution

Clubs do not pay the 8.33% first-band tax. Instead, under **s 3.6.2** the *required community
benefit contribution* is defined as **8⅓% of gaming revenue** for the financial year, and
**s 3.6.9** requires every club venue operator to prepare and lodge an **audited community
benefit statement** with the Commission by **30 September** following the financial year,
stating whether its spending on community purposes was less than, equal to, or greater than
that 8⅓%. **Pubs are expressly excluded** from this obligation (s 3.6.9(1A)).

The Commission **must publish every community benefit statement on the internet**
(s 3.6.9(6)). The Minister determines by Government Gazette order what counts as a
"community purpose" and may cap how much can be claimed per purpose (s 3.6.9(3), (3A)).

**This is a direct, addressable pot of money for a grassroots music project.** Club community
benefit spending is not appropriated through the budget — it is spent by the club itself,
against a statutory target, and audited and published. A club that funds local musicians is
meeting an obligation it has to meet anyway.

### Liquor licence renewal fees — 2026-27

Set under the **Liquor Control Reform Regulations 2023**, administered by Liquor Control
Victoria. Fee = **base fee + risk fees − star rating discount**. Fee units are re-set annually
by DTF.

**Base fees (2026-27):**

| Licence category | Base fee |
|---|---|
| Restricted club licence; Pre-retail licence | $195.80 |
| BYO permit; Full club; Restaurant and cafe; Remote seller's; Producer's; Renewable limited | $391.70 |
| **General; On-premises; Late night (general); Late night (on-premises)** | **$1,327.50** |
| Packaged liquor; Late night (packaged liquor) | $2,655.10 |

General licences in rural areas (Schedule 6, Modified Monash Model) get a 50% discount
automatically — not late night (general).

**Trading hours risk fees:**

| Licence type | Trading hours | Risk fee |
|---|---|---|
| Late night (general or on-premises) | 1am–3am | $7,861.30 |
| Late night (general or on-premises) | After 3am | $15,050.50 |
| Restaurant and cafe | After 1am | $3,562.30 |
| Packaged liquor / remote seller's | Outside standard hours | $11,633.80 |

**Venue capacity risk fees** (applies to general, on-premises and late night licences always;
to restaurant/cafe and club licences only if trading after 1am or carrying demerit points):

| Maximum patron capacity | Risk fee |
|---|---|
| **0 to 200 patrons** | **$0** |
| 201 to 400 | $1,227.60 |
| 401 to 800 | $2,778.60 |
| 801 to 1,000 | $8,635.00 |
| 1,001 or more | $21,725.70 |

**Non-compliance risk fees:** 1–2 demerit points in the past 12 months, $12,954.60; 3 or more,
$25,909.30; 1 or more point in the 13–36 month window, $6,477.30 (both can apply together).

**Star rating discounts:** every licence starts at 3 stars. 4 stars = 5% discount, 5 stars =
10%. Sexually explicit entertainment venues are assessed separately with a **minimum fee of
$44,320.00** and no star discount.

**What this means for a small live music pub:** a 150-capacity venue on an on-premises or
general licence that closes by 1am pays a **base fee of $1,327.50 and a $0 capacity risk
fee** — before any star discount. The Victorian liquor fee structure is already deliberately
tilted towards small, early-closing, compliant rooms. The expensive parts are lateness,
size, packaged sales and non-compliance. **There is no live-music concession or exemption in
the fee schedule.** Live music is fee-neutral: the state neither charges extra for it nor
discounts for it.

Venues in hardship can apply for a **waiver or reduction** by 31 March, but Liquor Control
Victoria cannot approve one if there has been any non-compliance incident in the previous
24 months. There is no live-music ground for waiver.

### Payroll tax

From the State Revenue Office, rates from 1 July 2025 onward:

- **Threshold (maximum deduction):** $1,000,000 annual / $83,333 monthly
- **Rate:** 4.85% metropolitan; **1.2125% regional Victorian employers**
- **Phase-out:** employers with total annual taxable Australian wages between $3,000,000 and
  $5,000,000 get a reduced deduction, phase-out rate 50% from 1 July 2025. Above $5,000,000,
  no deduction.
- **Surcharges** — both apply only where Australian wages exceed $10 million (first monthly
  threshold $833,333):
  - **Mental Health and Wellbeing Surcharge** — commenced 1 January 2022
  - **COVID-19 debt temporary payroll tax surcharge** — commenced 1 July 2023, **applies
    until 30 June 2033**
  - Combined: **1%** above $10m national payroll; **2%** above $100m. Applies only to the
    Victorian share of wages over the threshold.

**Reality check for the argument:** a single independent pub is almost never over the $1m
payroll tax threshold. Payroll tax is a hotel-group and pub-fund issue, not a
single-room-in-Collingwood issue. Budget Paper 5 forecasts payroll tax at **$10,077m** plus
$1,231m COVID Debt Levy plus $1,231m Mental Health and Wellbeing Levy in 2026-27 — but that
is the whole state economy, not hospitality.

## 1.2 Local councils

### Rate cap

The **Minister for Local Government** sets the council rate cap before 31 December each year
for the following financial year; the **Essential Services Commission** monitors compliance
and assesses higher-cap applications.

- **2025-26: 3.00%**
- **2026-27: 2.75%**

The cap applies to the *average* increase in general rates and municipal charges across the
whole rate base, not to any individual property, and councils can and do differentially rate
commercial and industrial property above residential.

### Agent of change

Victoria's **agent-of-change** principle sits in the state planning provisions and shifts
noise attenuation obligations onto whichever party is new to the situation — so a residential
development next to an existing live music venue bears the cost of soundproofing, not the
venue. This is the single most valuable non-cash protection the sector has won in Victoria.

## 1.3 Federal / ATO

Three federal streams flow off every pint and every ticket.

**GST — 10%.** Charged on the bar tab, on food, and on any door charge. The venue remits the
net of GST collected less input tax credits. For a live music room this is the single largest
federal impost by volume, because it applies to the whole of turnover rather than to profit.

**Company tax — 25% for a base rate entity, 30% otherwise.** Applies to profit, so a
marginal pub may pay little or none.

**Alcohol excise.** Paid by brewers, distillers and importers, not by the venue directly, but
it is embedded in every keg and bottle price the venue pays. Australian excise on beer and
spirits is levied **per litre of alcohol (LAL)** and is **indexed twice a year, in February
and August**, to the CPI. Beer in individual containers **over 48 litres** (i.e. kegs) is
taxed at a materially lower rate per litre of alcohol than beer in small packages — the
"draught beer" concession — which is why the on-premise keg trade is the cheapest way for a
consumer to buy taxed alcohol in Australia.

**[UNVERIFIED — current dollar rates.]** The exact current $/LAL figures for draught beer,
packaged beer and spirits could not be retrieved this pass (ato.gov.au blocks automated
access). Pull them from the ATO's *Excise duty rates for alcohol* page immediately before use
— they change every February and August, so any figure more than six months old is wrong.

**The point for the argument:** federal alcohol excise is the tax most obviously *earned* by
live music, because live music is what fills the room that empties the keg. None of it is
hypothecated to music. It is the clearest case of the sector generating tax revenue it sees
none of — but it is a federal argument, not one a Victorian MP or councillor can act on.

## 1.4 OneMusic Australia

OneMusic is the joint licensing venture of **APRA AMCOS and PPCA**, launched in 2019. It
issues the public performance licence a venue needs to play or host music. **The venue, not
the promoter, holds the licence in almost all cases** — OneMusic's own guidance says "the
authorising entity (the venue) is required to hold the licence in almost all cases," with a
list of national event promoters as the exception.

The relevant licence is the **Hotels, Pubs, Taverns, Bars and Casinos (HPTBC) scheme**. It is
not one fee — it is a stack of separate tariff heads:

**1. Background music (recorded, over the PA/TVs).** Six tiers — Bronze, Silver, Gold,
Platinum, Diamond, Sapphire — priced as a **flat fee** determined by the number of audible
televisions and music systems. Bronze is limited to a single television and a single radio;
Sapphire covers 10 or more televisions, any number of music systems, plus digital music
service rights. A deduction applies to any location open **fewer than 150 days per year**.
The rates originate in a 2006 Copyright Tribunal determination (CPI-adjusted), with the
sound-recording uplift negotiated with the Australian Hotels Association.

**2. Background music for dining.** Three packages (A, B, C), flat fee by dining area seating
capacity.

**3. Featured recorded music.** Flat fee, scaled by the number of persons admitted to the
featured-music area per day of operation per year.

**4. Recorded music for dance use.** An amount **per person admitted** to the dance area per
day of operation per year.

**5. Live Music Performance — this is the one that matters.**

> "The rate structure of Live Music Performance is a percentage of revenue, with the
> percentage rate depending on the method of payment to the live artist performer.
>
> If the live artist performer is paid a fee to perform directly by the venue, including any
> monies paid to the performer from the box office for that Live Music Performance, the rate
> is **2.2% of Gross Expenditure on live artist performers** for all relevant Live Music
> Performances.
>
> If the live artist performer is paid only by a third party, such as a booking agent, the
> rate is **1.65% of gross sums paid for admission** for all live music performances."

"Gross Expenditure" is defined broadly: all money and the monetary value of all benefits
receivable by the performer, including salaries, wages, profit shares, allowances,
accommodation and travel.

**6. Recorded music performance** (a live set incorporating recorded music the performer
didn't record — hip hop, drag). Same 2.2% / 1.65% APRA AMCOS component, **plus** a PPCA
per-person-admitted amount. If the entry fee exceeds $40 the venue is licensed under the
Events Scheme instead.

**7. Standalone functions.** Day rate per 100 persons of function area capacity, subject to a
minimum annual fee.

### What a small pub actually pays

**[UNVERIFIED — dollar figures.]** OneMusic's rate cards are behind bot protection and could
not be retrieved. Any claim of the form "a small pub pays roughly $X a year to OneMusic"
should not be made without the current HPTBC rate card in hand.

**But the structure is more useful than the number, and it is verified.** The live music
component of a OneMusic bill is **2.2% of what the venue pays the artists**. A venue that
pays its performers nothing pays nothing under the live music tariff head. A venue that pays
a band $400 pays OneMusic $8.80. The only fixed, unavoidable OneMusic cost for a pub is the
**background music** tariff — the jukebox and the TVs — which is charged whether or not a
single musician ever walks in.

**This is a genuinely strong advocacy fact and it is not widely known.** The music licensing
system does not penalise live music. It charges a small percentage of artist spend, and it
charges most of its money for *recorded* music. "OneMusic fees make live music unaffordable"
is not supported by the tariff structure. What makes live music unaffordable is that the
venue must pay the artist first — and the 2.2% is a rounding error on top of that.

# Part 2 — Where the money comes back out

## 2.1 The Community Support Fund

### How it is funded

**Gambling Regulation Act 2003 (Vic) s 3.6.12.** An amount equal to the *relevant pub gaming
machine entitlement amount* must be paid out of the Consolidated Fund into the Community
Support Fund, and the Consolidated Fund is appropriated to the necessary extent for those
payments (s 3.6.12(2)).

Section 3.6.12(3) defines it:

> *relevant pub gaming machine entitlement amount* means an amount equal to **8⅓%** of the
> product of the sum of all average revenue per gaming machine entitlement earned by venue
> operators and the sum of all of the average number of gaming machine entitlements...

So: **8⅓% of pub (hotel) electronic gaming machine revenue**, plus interest earned on the
fund balance (s 10.3.2(2)). The Department of Treasury and Finance confirms this plainly:
the CSF "receives 8.33% of revenue from electronic gaming machines in hotels, plus any
interest earned on the fund balance."

**Clubs do not contribute.** They are excluded because they carry the mirror-image
obligation to spend 8⅓% of gaming revenue on community purposes themselves, under the
community benefit statement regime in s 3.6.9 (see Part 1.1). The 8.33% appears twice in
Victorian law for the same reason: it is the standing price of a pokies licence, paid to the
state by pubs and paid to the community directly by clubs.

The Fund was established in 1991 under s 138 of the *Gaming Machine Control Act 1991* and
continues under s 10.3.1 of the current Act.

### What the CSF is legally required to fund

**Section 10.3.3** sets a strict order of priority. The Minister may apply money in the Fund:

**(a) first**, for any or all of:
  1. research on the social and economic impact of gambling, causes of problem gambling and
     harm minimisation strategies, and publication of that research;
  2. programs for the prevention of excessive gambling, or treatment/rehabilitation of
     problem gamblers;
  3. payments into the **Gambling Harm Response Fund**.

**(b) secondly**, for any one or more of:
  1. treatment or rehabilitation of drug addiction;
  2. drug education programs;
  3. financial counselling services, or support and assistance for families in crisis;
  4. **programs for the benefit of youth**;
  5. research or pilot programs relating to community advancement programs;
  6. **programs for the benefit of sport or recreation**;
  7. **"programs for the promotion or benefit of the arts"** — s 10.3.3(1)(b)(vii);
  8. programs establishing or developing tourist destinations, facilities or services, or
     promoting tourism;
  9. **any other programs or purposes relating to the support or advancement of the community
     as determined by the Minister**.

**(c) thirdly**, administration and management of the Fund.

Additionally, **s 10.3.3A** requires the Minister to pay the **Victorian Veterans Fund** one
day's worth of the preceding year's EGM revenue into the Fund, every 1 September.

**The arts are already a named statutory purpose of the Community Support Fund.** No
legislative change is needed to fund a grassroots live music project from the CSF. The
authority exists in s 10.3.3(1)(b)(vii) and has since the Act commenced.

### Can you apply? No — and this is the crux

**You cannot apply to the Community Support Fund.** DTF states plainly that *"the Department
of Treasury and Finance does not provide CSF grants directly to applicants."* Organisations
must approach the **relevant government portfolio department**, which administers approved
funding and distributes grants.

In practice the CSF is not a grants program at all. It is a **hypothecated revenue stream
that is appropriated to departments through the annual budget process**. The money arrives
in a department's output funding and is then spent through whatever program that department
runs. By the time it reaches a musician it is called something else entirely — a Creative
Victoria grant, a youth program, a council partnership.

**The practical consequence for advocacy:** asking the government to "fund grassroots music
from the CSF" is not a request for a grant. It is a request for a **budget allocation
decision** — that the department administering creative industries receive a CSF-funded
output line for grassroots live music. That is a request you make to a Minister, a Treasurer,
or through the budget submission process, **not** through any grants portal.

## 2.4 Is anything hypothecated to the arts?

**Gaming revenue: partially, and the arts are named — but you cannot reach it directly.**

- 8⅓% of pub EGM revenue is hypothecated by statute to the Community Support Fund
  (s 3.6.12). ~$161m in 2026-27.
- The CSF's statutory purposes **expressly include "programs for the promotion or benefit of
  the arts"** (s 10.3.3(1)(b)(vii)) — but only in the *second* order of priority, after
  gambling harm research, treatment and the Gambling Harm Response Fund are satisfied.
- The CSF is appropriated to departments through the budget. There is no application path.
- 81.9% of *all* gambling tax revenue in 2026-27 goes to the Hospitals and Charities Fund,
  the Mental Health Fund and the CSF combined — with health taking the overwhelming majority
  ($1,258.6m under s 3.6.11 alone versus $161.2m to the CSF).
- Separately, clubs must direct **8⅓% of their gaming revenue to community purposes** and
  lodge audited, publicly-published community benefit statements. **This is directly
  addressable by an outside organisation** — you approach the club, not the government.

**Liquor licence revenue: essentially no.** Liquor licence fees raise $37m in 2026-27 and go
to the Consolidated Fund as general revenue. Budget Paper 5 shows a single standing special
appropriation under the *Liquor Control Reform Act 1998* s 177(2) of **$6.0 million per year**
(flat, 2025-26 and 2026-27). Nothing in the liquor revenue chain is hypothecated to arts or
music.

**Racing: yes, and generously.** The *Gambling Taxation Act 2023* s 33 hypothecates a
**Wagering to Victorian Racing Industry Payment** of **$181.6 million in 2026-27** — more
than the entire Community Support Fund. Racing has what music does not: a named,
legislated, indexed share of gambling revenue.

# Part 3 — The argument

*This is the case. Every figure is sourced in Part 1 and Part 2. It makes no claim about any
individual venue's tax affairs.*

---

## The one-paragraph version

Victoria will collect **$1.567 billion** in electronic gaming machine tax in 2026-27 and
another **$37 million** in liquor licence fees. In the same budget, all contemporary music in
Victoria receives **$4.5 million** — a single-year allocation with nothing in the forward
estimates. That is **0.29 per cent** of the pokies take. Live music in Victoria was a
**$2.54 billion** sector across 184,043 gigs in 1,076 venues before the pandemic, and in the
small-venue tier — the pubs, the back rooms, the free shows — **83 per cent of all revenue
generated was money spent at the bar, not at the door**. Musicians are the unpaid input to a
taxed output. The state already has the statutory power to fund them: the Community Support
Fund, funded by 8⅓ per cent of pub pokies revenue, expressly lists *"programs for the
promotion or benefit of the arts"* among its purposes. It simply doesn't use it.

---

## 1. The scale of the tax the sector's host industry pays

| Stream | 2026-27 | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Electronic gaming machine tax | **$1,567m** | BP5 Table 4.2 |
| All gambling taxes | **~$2,900m** | BP5 Ch. 4 |
| Liquor licence fees | **$37m** | BP5 Table 4.2 |
| Community Support Fund appropriation | $161.2m | BP5 Appendix A.4 |
| **Contemporary music initiatives** | **$4.5m** | **BP3 Table 1.17** |

Ratios worth quoting out loud:

- EGM tax is **348 times** the contemporary music allocation.
- The Community Support Fund alone is **36 times** the contemporary music allocation.
- The **Victorian Racing Industry Payment ($181.6m)** is **40 times** the contemporary music
  allocation, and is guaranteed by statute rather than granted year to year.
- Contemporary music ($4.5m) receives **less than a quarter** of what the Victorian Screen
  Incentive receives in the same budget ($17.2m + $10.0m over two years), and **less than a
  fifth** of the Melbourne Arts Precinct Transformation line ($20.7m + $8.3m).

None of this is an argument that screen or the arts precinct are overfunded. It is an
argument that grassroots contemporary music is being funded as a rounding error inside a
portfolio that is itself dwarfed by the gambling revenue extracted from the same rooms where
that music is played.

## 2. The venues genuinely do profit from live music — and the money is at the bar

From the **Victorian Live Music Census 2021-22** (Music Victoria), reporting 2019 as the last
year of normal activity:

**Statewide:**

| Metric | 2019 |
|---|---|
| Live music venues in Victoria | 1,076 |
| Live music gigs | 184,043 |
| Total live music audience | 50.15 million |
| Box office / entry revenue | $638.8m |
| Onsite attendee spending | $1.42 billion |
| Offsite attendee spending | $235.1m |
| **Total event/gig value** | **$2.54 billion** (up from $1.72bn in 2017) |
| Full-time-equivalent gig/event jobs | 9,165 |
| Victorian songwriters | 32,048 |

**Small venues specifically — the pubs and back rooms:**

- **48,120 shows**, **15.1 million patrons**, **$1,189 million total revenue** in 2019
- Of that $1,189m:
  - **Box office: $141.2m** (regional $17.6m + metro $123.6m) — **11.9%**
  - **On-site spend: $984.0m** (regional $160.1m + metro $823.9m) — **82.7%**
  - Off-site spend: $64.5m — 5.4%
- **Approximately 60% of shows presented in small venues are free entry.**
- 505 metropolitan and 308 regional venues were identified as *regular* presenters —
  a minimum of one gig per week.

**This is the argument in one line: for every dollar generated by a gig in a small Victorian
venue, roughly 83 cents is spent at the bar and 12 cents at the door.** The venue captures
the 83 cents. The state takes GST on all of it and excise is already embedded in every keg.
The musician, if there is a door at all, splits the 12 cents — and in 60 per cent of shows
there is no door at all.

The room is not doing the artist a favour by putting on a free gig. The free gig is what
fills the room.

## 3. What each level of government does and doesn't get back

**State.** Takes $1,567m in EGM tax and $37m in liquor licence fees. Returns $4.5m to
contemporary music, for one year. Has a statutory arts purpose sitting unused inside a
$161m fund that it controls entirely.

**Federal.** Takes 10% GST on the whole bar tab and door, and collects alcohol excise on every
keg that live music helps empty — indexed twice a year, every year. Returns nothing
hypothecated to music.

**Local.** Takes rates, permit fees and the ESVF collection, and is the **only** level of
government that runs a program a grassroots music night can actually apply to and win.
Yarra puts $1.54m on the table. Merri-bek will give an individual artist $6,000 with no
auspicing required. Melbourne will give $25,000 for a public outcome in the CBD.

**Councils are doing more, per dollar of revenue, for grassroots music than the state is —
and they have a 2.75% rate cap while the state collects $1.567 billion from pokies.**

## 4. The three specific asks

These are the asks that follow from the evidence, in order of how hard they are.

### Ask 1 — to a councillor, achievable now

*Expand the small/quick-response grant stream and make recurring live music explicitly
eligible.*

Yarra's Small grants (up to $4,000, rolling, volunteer-led, under 50 participants, minimal
reporting) are already the right shape for a grassroots music night. Merri-bek's individual
artist grant ($6,000, no auspicing) is the right shape for a musician. Neither is designed
around a **recurring** night that pays **other people**. The ask is not new money — it is a
line in the guidelines confirming that a series of gigs paying local artists is an eligible
project, and that the artist fees are an eligible cost.

Also ask for what costs nothing: **waive or discount the local permits** a live music venue
needs — footpath trading, outdoor dining, amplified entertainment — for venues that
demonstrably present live music and pay their artists.

### Ask 2 — to an MP, requires a budget decision but no legislation

*Create a standing, CSF-funded grassroots live music line under s 10.3.3(1)(b)(vii).*

The Community Support Fund already lists **"programs for the promotion or benefit of the
arts"** as a statutory purpose. It holds ~$161m a year, funded by 8⅓% of pub pokies revenue.
No Act needs to change. What is needed is a budget allocation: a **recurring output line for
grassroots live music**, funded from the CSF, administered by the department responsible for
creative industries.

The precedent is on the table in the same budget document. **Racing has a legislated,
recurring $181.6m payment from wagering tax.** Music has a discretionary $4.5m for one year.
The ask is for the *form* racing has, at a fraction of the *scale*.

To be honest about the counter-argument: BP5 forecasts EGM revenue to decline from 2028-29
as harm minimisation measures take effect. A funding line indexed to pokies revenue is a
funding line that shrinks. If that is politically or ethically uncomfortable — and it should
be — the answer is to argue for the allocation to be set as a **fixed recurring sum**, not a
share, so that reducing gambling harm doesn't defund musicians.

### Ask 3 — to clubs directly, requires nobody's permission

*Fund local musicians out of your community benefit obligation.*

Every club venue operator in Victoria must apply **8⅓% of gaming revenue** to community
purposes and lodge an **audited community benefit statement** by 30 September each year. The
Commission **must publish every one of those statements on the internet**. The Minister
determines by Gazette order what counts as a community purpose.

This money is not appropriated through a budget. It is spent by the club, against a target
it must hit and report on. A club that funds a monthly local music night, pays the artists,
and books it in its community benefit statement is **meeting an obligation it already has**,
in a way that is visibly local and easy to defend.

Two things follow. First, approach clubs directly with a costed proposal — the money exists
and the statement is public, so you can see what they are already claiming. Second, a
legitimate advocacy ask to the Minister is that the Gazette order determining community
purposes **explicitly name payment of professional fees to local performing artists** as a
qualifying community purpose.

## 5. The honest framing

Do not overclaim. The claims that hold up:

- The state raises enormous revenue from gambling in licensed venues, and returns a
  negligible fraction to contemporary music. **Verified: $1,567m vs $4.5m.**
- The statutory authority to fund the arts from gambling revenue already exists and is
  unused. **Verified: s 10.3.3(1)(b)(vii).**
- Grassroots live music generates the bar spend that is the overwhelming majority of small
  venue revenue, and mostly does so without a door charge. **Verified: 82.7% on-site spend,
  ~60% of small venue shows free entry.**
- Music licensing is not the barrier. **Verified: the live music tariff is 2.2% of what the
  venue pays artists — it scales with artist pay rather than suppressing it.**
- Councils are the only tier with an open door. **Verified: Yarra, Merri-bek, Melbourne
  programs above.**

The claims that do **not** hold up and should not be made:

- "Venues are being taxed out of existence by liquor licence fees." A ≤200-capacity venue
  closing by 1am pays a $1,327.50 base fee and a $0 capacity risk fee. The fee structure
  already favours exactly the kind of room this sector wants to protect.
- "OneMusic fees kill live music." The live music tariff is a percentage of artist spend.
- "Payroll tax is crushing small venues." The threshold is $1m in wages; most independent
  single-site venues are well under it.
- Anything about a named venue's tax position. Don't.

The strongest version of this case is not that venues are being gouged. It is that
**musicians are the unpaid input to a heavily taxed output, and the state has a fund with an
arts purpose written into it that it has chosen not to spend on them.**
