# Musicians' rights and fair pay — the standards we hold venues to

Researched 2026-07-29. Sources at the bottom. This document exists so the
directory's rankings rest on published industry standards rather than our
opinion. That matters twice over: it is more persuasive to a venue, and it is a
far stronger position if anyone ever threatens to sue over a rating.

---

## 1. The benchmark number

**$250 per musician, per performance, for a gig of 3 hours or less.**

This is the Musicians Australia (MEAA) minimum fee. Set in early 2021 by member
vote. It is described by the union as a floor that cannot be undercut, "for any
musician, playing anywhere, for any audience".

Where the figure comes from: the Live Performance Award's "3 hour call"
provision, which sets roughly $150 to $200 base pay plus $50 to $100 in
allowances for setup, meals, instruments and travel.

Endorsed for grant-funded gigs by the South Australian, Queensland, Western
Australian, **Victorian** and ACT governments.

Longer than 3 hours warrants more. Artists are encouraged to add a fee on top
based on market and reputation.

**Why this is the right anchor for us:** it is not our number. When a venue
argues, the answer is "that is the union's floor and your own state government
endorses it", not "I reckon you should pay more".

**Honest caveat for open mics specifically:** a 15-minute open mic slot is not a
3-hour engagement, and nobody sensibly claims $250 for two songs. The $250
figure governs *booked gigs*, which is Stage 2 of the plan, when donn is
booking acts. For open mics the relevant standards are different, and they are
in section 3.

## 2. The legal frame, briefly

- **Live Performance Award 2020 (MA000081)** is the modern award covering live
  performance work. It applies where the musician is an employee. Most pub gigs
  are structured as independent contracting, which puts them outside the award,
  which is exactly why the union set a voluntary floor instead.
- **ABN withholding.** Where a business pays a contractor who does not quote an
  ABN, the payer generally must withhold 47%. Relevant the moment donn starts
  paying acts for his own nights. Get an accountant on this before the first
  payment, not after.
- **OneMusic Australia** now handles venue music licensing, the joint APRA AMCOS
  and PPCA scheme. A venue hosting live music needs a licence. Music Victoria's
  Best Practice Guidelines devote a chapter to it.
- **Music Victoria Best Practice Guidelines for Live Music Venues** is the
  sector's own standard. Members-only, so we cannot quote it directly, but its
  existence is useful: we are asking venues to meet a standard their own peak
  body already publishes. Worth a Music Victoria membership to read it properly.

## 3. What "fair" means at an open mic

The $250 floor does not fit a two-song slot. These do, and they are what the
directory measures:

| Standard | Why it is fair | How we record it |
|---|---|---|
| Performers who play get a drink, or its value | They brought an audience and filled your room for free | Yes / No, venue-set |
| The jar is split evenly and openly | Tips are for performers, not the house | Split shown to every performer |
| Slot times are honoured within 30 min | People rearrange their night around it | Timing captured automatically |
| No pay-to-play, ever | See section 4 | Reported by performers |
| No cut of artist merch | The venue did not make the merch | Yes / No |
| A working PA and someone who can run it | It is a performance, not a karaoke machine | Yes / No |
| Written terms before the night | Verbal deals get forgotten in the venue's favour | Yes / No |

## 4. Ticket sales, and the practice worth exposing

donn asked specifically about ticket sales. The exploitative pattern is
well known across the industry:

**Pay-to-play.** An artist is given tickets to sell, or required to pre-sell a
quota, and only gets paid, or only keeps their slot, if they shift enough.
The financial risk of promoting the night is shifted onto the least
resourced party in the room. Where the artist has to buy the tickets up front,
they can lose money by performing.

This is the thing the directory should name plainly, because it is the single
practice that does most damage to new artists and it is invisible from the
outside.

**Fair ticketing models, for contrast:**
- A guarantee, or a door split, whichever is greater
- A transparent door count the artist can see
- Door takings minus agreed, itemised costs, split at a stated percentage
- Costs disclosed in writing before the night, not deducted after

**What we measure:** was there a ticket quota, was the door count visible, were
costs agreed in writing beforehand. Facts, reported by multiple performers,
with right of reply.

MEAA's own campaign line is that a $9 billion industry should stop exploiting
professional musicians. We are not making a radical claim. We are pointing a
torch at the bottom of an industry that already agrees with us in principle.

## 5. The APRA feature — the biggest pay-it-forward in this whole project

This is worth more to a grassroots artist than the drink and the jar combined,
and almost nobody at open mic level knows it exists.

**How the money works.** APRA AMCOS collects licence fees from pubs, hotels and
restaurants that host live music. Those fees go into a live performance
distribution pool. The pool is paid out **by direct allocation to performance
reports that members submit** — an online form listing the original songs they
performed live, where and when. Paid quarterly. A standard value is applied per
performance.

**The consequence nobody spells out:** if you do not file a report, you do not
get paid, and the pool is shared among the people who did. The pub pays its
licence fee either way. Grassroots artists are topping up a pool that gets
distributed to whoever does the paperwork, which skews to the organised and the
established.

**What our system already has:** who performed, at which venue, on which date.
That is most of a performance report. The only missing field is the song
titles.

**The feature:** after a performer's set, ask for their song titles. Generate a
ready-to-submit APRA performance report. One tap, and they claim money they
were already owed and were never going to see.

**Honest caveats, which must be on the screen, not buried:**
- **Originals only.** If you played covers, the royalty goes to whoever wrote
  the song, not to you. Most open mic sets are covers, so this will not apply to
  everyone.
- **You must be an APRA writer member** to submit a report and be paid. Check
  the current joining requirements and fee before we tell anyone it is free.
- Songs not already in APRA's database can be registered at the same time the
  report is submitted.
- We generate the report. The artist submits it. We never submit on someone's
  behalf and we never touch the money.

**Why this is the David and Goliath move:** it costs us nothing, it takes money
from nobody who earned it, and it routes existing industry money back down to
the people the industry is built on. It also cannot be copied by a venue-side
product, because no venue wants to be the one telling artists to claim.

## 6. How this maps to the directory

Each criterion in section 3 and 4 becomes a factual field, reported by
performers, aggregated, never a single person's word. Every venue gets right of
reply published alongside. The method page cites this document and the sources
below, so the standard being applied is visible and external.

Stated editorial position, published: **venues that support local artists rank
above venues that put profit first.** That is a values position, openly
declared, the same way any magazine's best-of list is. It is not neutral and
does not claim to be.
