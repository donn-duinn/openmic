## 1. Letter to MPs, councillors and ministers

Adapt the opening line per recipient. Keep it to two pages when printed —
electorate offices do not read more.

> **[NAME], [TITLE]**
> [ELECTORATE OFFICE ADDRESS]
>
> Dear [NAME],
>
> **Re: grassroots live music in [ELECTORATE] — what I've seen, and four things
> that would fix it**
>
> I'm a working musician based in Melbourne. I play the small rooms: the open
> mics and new-artist nights in [SUBURBS]. I'm also a self-taught AI and
> software practitioner, operating as a sole trader (ABN 69 173 867 628). No
> formal training in either — what I have instead is a working system you can
> inspect.
>
> Last month I built a free sign-up system for these nights and started giving
> it to venues at no cost, because I kept watching good nights run badly. It's
> live, it's free permanently, and the source is public under an open licence.
>
> I'm writing because building it showed me how much of this sector runs on
> nothing, and how little would be needed to change that.
>
> And because I can't do this on my own. I can build the thing and give it
> away. I can't reach every venue in Melbourne from a standing start, and I
> can't fix the reasons those venues stopped paying artists in the first place.
> That part needs government, and it needs people like you. That's the whole
> reason for this letter.
>
> **What I've seen first-hand**
>
> At one Melbourne venue this month, more than fifty performers signed up for a
> single night through an emailed link. Not a form. A link, and one person
> trying to keep order in their inbox while running a room. That venue is not
> unusual and it is not badly run. It is normal.
>
> Across roughly forty-five Melbourne rooms I've catalogued, sign-ups are
> handled by a clipboard, a Facebook comment thread, or a direct message.
> Melbourne's main open mic comedy listing tells performers in writing to
> "reach out (probably via Facebook)" to get on a list. That is the state of
> the infrastructure under the country's most celebrated music city.
>
> **What follows from that, and this is the part that matters**
>
> Because nothing is recorded, nothing can be checked. Nobody knows how many
> artists played in this electorate last year, what they were paid, or whether
> they were paid at all. There is no data, so there is no accountability, and
> so poor practice survives by default rather than by intent. Most venue
> operators I've met are not hostile to artists. They are busy, and no system
> has ever asked them to keep a record.
>
> The consequences are well documented by the sector's own bodies:
>
> - Musicians Australia (MEAA) set a minimum fee of **$250 per musician** for a
>   performance of up to three hours, derived from the Live Performance Award's
>   three-hour call. The Victorian Government already endorses this standard for
>   grant-funded gigs. Below that level, in the rooms I play, it is largely
>   aspirational.
> - MEAA's public position is that a nine-billion-dollar industry should stop
>   exploiting professional musicians.
> - APRA AMCOS distributes live performance royalties by direct allocation to
>   performance reports that members file. Artists who don't file don't get
>   paid, and the pool goes to those who do. Grassroots performers are
>   overwhelmingly in the first group. Venues pay the licence fee either way.
> - Sound engineers and photographers at this level are routinely offered
>   credit instead of a fee.
>
> **Four things that would change it**
>
> 1. **Recognise grassroots nights in live music funding.** Current support
>    skews to established venues, festivals and touring. The open mic is where
>    every one of those artists started, and it receives close to nothing.
>
> 2. **Make paying local artists an explicit, encouraged community purpose in
>    Community Benefit Statement guidance.** Victorian club venue operators must
>    already direct at least 8.33 per cent of net gaming machine revenue to
>    community benefit each year, and lodge an audited statement by 30
>    September. If clubs in this electorate are already obliged to spend that
>    money, paying local musicians should be plainly listed as a way to do it.
>
> 3. **Back the information gap directly.** Most performers do not know the
>    union minimum exists, that APRA may owe them money, or that Support Act
>    runs a free 24-hour helpline. They are not careless. They are musicians,
>    and nobody has ever told them. I've built that information into the point
>    where they sign up. It costs nothing to run and reaches every performer who
>    walks in.
>
> 4. **Fund the record-keeping, not another platform.** The reason nobody can
>    answer "what are artists paid in Victoria" is that no one has ever
>    collected it. A modest amount of support would produce, for the first time,
>    real numbers on grassroots live performance in this state.
>
> **The bigger thing behind all of this**
>
> I want to say plainly what I think is happening underneath.
>
> A pub has a finite room and a finite floor. Over the last thirty years, in a
> great many Victorian venues, the room that held a band came to hold gaming
> machines instead. That is a rational decision for an operator: a gaming room
> earns predictably and a band does not. But it was a decision made venue by
> venue, with no one accounting for what it cost the state as a whole, and the
> people who paid for it were musicians and the audiences who would have been
> in that room.
>
> Meanwhile the costs of running a small venue — rates, licensing, compliance,
> insurance, staffing — keep climbing, and the first line cut when a venue is
> squeezed is always live music, because it is the only cost that feels
> optional.
>
> The result is a state that markets itself internationally on live music while
> the ground floor of that scene runs on volunteers, clipboards and unpaid
> work.
>
> I am not asking you to take a position on gambling policy. I am asking you to
> notice that grassroots live music has no data, no floor and no advocate at
> the level where it actually happens, and that this is fixable cheaply.
>
> **What I'm asking of you**
>
> Three things, and the first is the one I most want.
>
> 1. **Guide me.** I am a musician who has built something useful and I do not
>    know how this system works. Tell me who to talk to, which door to knock on,
>    what I am getting wrong, and what has already been tried and failed. Half an
>    hour of your staff's time would save me a year.
>
> 2. **Help me put it in every pub that wants it.** It is free, it stays free,
>    and it is open source. What I lack is reach and standing. A word from you,
>    or an introduction to your council's arts team or to Music Victoria, opens
>    doors that a cold email from a musician does not.
>
> 3. **Consider the four changes above**, particularly making artist payment an
>    explicit community purpose for club venues that already must spend that
>    money somewhere.
>
> I'm not asking for money in this letter. I've already built the thing and
> given it away. I'm happy to demonstrate it in person, at your office or at a
> venue in the electorate, whichever is more useful.
>
> I've done the part I know how to do. I can't do the rest alone, and I'd
> rather ask for help early than spend a year finding that out.
>
> Yours sincerely,

> system for open mic and new-artist nights, and I'm giving it to venues at no

## 2b. The contradiction to resolve before sending both

These two letters currently argue opposite things about gambling.

The **club letter** asks venues to fund musicians out of gaming revenue, using
the community benefit obligation. The **MP letter** argues that gaming rooms
displaced band rooms and hollowed out grassroots live music.

Both positions are genuinely held by serious people in this sector. Held at the
same time, by the same person, in the same month, they read as incoherent, and
a staffer will notice.

**Options, pick one:**

1. **Lead with the displacement argument to MPs, and drop the club letter.**
   Cleanest position, hardest on funding. You become an advocate, not a
   partner.
2. **Lead with the club letter, and soften the MP letter to "the sector is
   unfunded" without the gambling causation.** Easier money, weaker politics,
   and you can't later campaign on it.
3. **Sequence it.** Clubs first, quietly, to get nights funded and running.
   Build the record. Make the structural argument later, from evidence, once
   the not-for-profit exists and it isn't your personal name carrying it.

Option 3 is probably right, but it is a real choice with a real cost, and it
should be made deliberately rather than by accident of which letter goes out
first.

**Also unverified:** the claim that gaming machines displaced live music rooms
in Victorian pubs is widely made and plausible, but nothing in this repo
sources it yet. Find the research before putting it in front of an MP. Music
Victoria or a parliamentary inquiry submission is the likely source.

## 3. Homework before either letter goes out

- **Verify the community benefit claim.** Confirm with the VGCCC that paying
  local artists is claimable, and under which category. The published guidance
  lists donations, sponsorships and subsidised activities as direct community
  benefits, but does not explicitly name arts. **Do not put this in front of a
  club until it's confirmed.** Being wrong here would be embarrassing and would
  cost the relationship.
- **Read the published statements.** Community Benefit Statements are public,
  back to 2003-04, summarised by local government area. Look up what clubs in
  Merri-bek, Darebin, Yarra and Moonee Valley have claimed. Walk in knowing
  their numbers.
- **Get the contact list.** No MP, councillor or club contact details have been
  gathered yet.
- **Custom domain.** A letter to a Member of Parliament linking to a
  `workers.dev` address undercuts everything above it.

## 4. On sending

Same rule as the venue outreach, and for the same reasons.

MPs' offices exist to be written to, so there's no spam issue and the letter is
low-risk. But it goes out under Daniel's name, with his ABN on it, making
factual claims about a sector he wants to work in for the next twenty years. It
was drafted at 3am by an AI at the end of a long night.

**He reads it in daylight, corrects the voice until it sounds like him, and
sends it himself.** The parts that make it good are the first-hand
observations, and those are his.

Suggested order: the local state MP and the councillor for the ward he lives
in first. Two letters, real replies, then adjust and widen. Forty identical
letters sent at once get forty form responses.
