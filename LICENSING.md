# Licensing — what's protected and how

Three different tools, because "open source but they can't copy it" needs
three, not one.

## The code: AGPL-3.0

`LICENSE` is the GNU Affero General Public License v3.

Anyone may use, modify and run this. **But if they run a modified version as a
network service, they must publish their source.** That is the clause that
matters, and it is why AGPL rather than MIT or GPL.

**What it stops:** a hospitality software company taking this, closing it,
adding a subscription, and selling it back to the venues we gave it to. They
can still do it — but their version has to stay open too, which removes the
entire commercial reason to bother.

**What it does not stop:** another musician in Brisbane running their own copy
for their own scene. That is not a threat, that is the point.

Changed from MIT on 2026-07-29. MIT permitted exactly the closed-source capture
we are trying to prevent.

## The documents: CC BY-SA 4.0

`FAIR-PAY.md`, and any published version of the venue standards, are Creative
Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0.

Reuse them, translate them, adapt them for Sydney or Perth. Credit the source
and keep derivatives under the same licence. Standards want to spread. A fair
pay standard that only one city uses is not a standard.

## The name: trademark

This is the real "they cannot copy this".

Code is copyable by design and by licence. A name is not. Registering the
project and Tech Duinn word marks with IP Australia is what actually stops
someone running a competing thing under our name and inheriting the trust we
built.

Costs a few hundred dollars per class. Classes likely relevant: 42 (software
services), 41 (entertainment and cultural activities). **Not yet done.** Worth
doing before any public launch, because filing after someone else is expensive
and sometimes impossible.

## What was deliberately not used

**Ethical source licences** (Hippocratic, Anti-Capitalist Software Licence and
similar) ban specified bad actors or uses. They are appealing and they are a
bad fit here:

- They are not OSI-approved open source, which makes the project ineligible for
  some grants, and awkward for councils and public bodies to adopt
- They are largely untested in court, so the protection may be illusory
- They create legal uncertainty for exactly the small, well-meaning operators
  we want reusing this

AGPL gives real, tested, enforceable protection against the specific threat.
Values belong in the documents and the governance, not in an unenforceable
clause.

## Contributor terms

Contributions are accepted under AGPL-3.0. If the project later incorporates as
a not-for-profit, a lightweight contributor licence agreement may be needed so
the entity can hold and defend the copyright. Not required yet, flagged for
when incorporation happens.
