# Media approach

## The hook

Not "local musician builds app". That is not a story and no editor will run it.

**The story is the money.** Every pub that hosts live music pays a licence fee.
APRA AMCOS distributes that pool to whoever files a performance report. Artists
who never file are funding a pool they never claim from — and APRA's own
guidance explicitly names open mic nights as eligible.

Nobody has ever built the bridge between the sign-up sheet and the royalty
claim. APRA's own app has listed setlist creation as "coming soon" since 2020.
Six years. The entire competing field is one GitHub repository with two commits.

**The angle:** Australia's grassroots musicians are leaving money on the table
that the industry collects on their behalf, and the reason is that nobody told
them. A Melbourne musician built the missing piece in one night and gave it
away.

Secondary angles, depending on outlet:
- **Tech/AI:** a worked example of AI used to transfer value to low-paid workers
  rather than displace them. See `ETHICAL-AI.md`.
- **Industry/policy:** Victorian club venues must direct at least 8.33% of net
  gaming machine revenue to community benefit annually. Paying local musicians
  is an arguable use of money they must already spend.
- **Human:** fifty performers signing up for one night through an emailed link.

## The pitch email

Short. Journalists get hundreds.

> Subject: Melbourne musicians are leaving APRA royalties unclaimed — and
> nobody built the fix
>
> Hi [NAME],
>
> Quick one, and I think it's a story.
>
> Every pub that hosts live music pays a licence fee. APRA distributes that
> money to whoever files a performance report listing what they played, where
> and when. Their own guidance says open mic nights count.
>
> Almost nobody at that level files. So grassroots musicians are funding a pool
> that gets paid out to people with better admin.
>
> I'm a Melbourne musician. Last month I watched fifty performers sign up for
> one open mic through an emailed link. I built a free sign-up system to fix
> that — and realised it already captures most of what an APRA report needs.
>
> It's live, it's free forever, it's open source, and I don't make anything from
> it. It also tells performers the union minimum fee exists, splits the tip jar
> evenly, and puts the Support Act helpline in front of everyone who signs up.
>
> Happy to show you, or to put you in touch with venues and performers using it.
>

## Write it yourself, which is the one thing you can do now

Beat publishes pieces written by people in the scene: `submissions@beat.com.au`
is for story ideas and submissions, not just coverage requests.

That matters because it is the one approach that does not depend on venue
numbers. A journalist writing about you needs traction to justify the piece. An
argument written by you needs to be right, and it already is: the APRA pool that
grassroots artists fund and never claim from, the $250 floor almost nobody has
heard of, and 83 cents in every gig dollar going over the bar. All sourced, all
in [`FAIR-PAY.md`](FAIR-PAY.md) and the business plan.

**The piece to write:** *"Musicians are funding a royalty pool they never claim
from, and nobody told them."* First person, from the room, 800 words, with the
APRA guidance quoted and the sources listed. Do not make it about the software.
Mention it once at the end.

Same applies to the policy outlets, where the piece is *"AI cannot do live
music, so fund the AI that does the paperwork"* rather than anything about open
mics.

## What is missing before pitching

**Do not pitch yet.** The story needs one thing it does not have: someone other
than donn using it.

A journalist will ask "which venues?" and "how many artists have claimed?" If
the answer is none, there is no story and you have burned the contact. Music
journalists are a small pool and you get one first approach each.

Wait for: three venues running it weekly, and ideally one artist who has filed
an APRA report and been paid. That second one is the whole story in one person.

Then it writes itself.

## On volume

Five personalised pitches to people who cover this beat will do more than two
hundred generic ones. A mass send to a media list is identifiable on sight and
marks the sender as someone to ignore permanently.

Same for MPs. Your own federal member, the federal Arts Minister, the Victorian
Creative Industries Minister, and any open inquiry accepting submissions.
Submissions to live inquiries are public, permanent, and read by more people
than a letter ever will.
